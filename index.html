Station 02 · Materials science

Stress, strain & material behaviour

Stress is the applied force per area; strain describes deformation relative to original length.

Mini-lab

Stretch test: change the load

This simple model uses a bar with a fixed cross-sectional area. Watch the bar elongate as the force increases.

2.0 kN
Stress = F ÷ A → 2.00 MPa (for this visual: area = 10 cm²)

Three types of stress

Compression

Opposing inward forces squeeze a material.

Tension

Opposing outward forces pull a material apart.

Shear

A force parallel to a surface makes parts of the material slide or distort.

Elastic

Returns to its original shape when the force is removed.

Plastic

Permanent deformation; it does not fully return to its original shape.

Creep · fracture · brittle

Creep: slow permanent deformation under constant stress over time. Fracture: sudden breakage. Brittle: breaks with little plastic deformation.

Keep cause and effect separate

Stress, σ = F / A
Strain, ε = ΔL / L₀

Stress is measured in Pa (N·m⁻²). Strain is a ratio, so it has no unit. A stressed material may deform; if it does, it is under strain.

Worked check

If a force of 2.0 kN acts on 10 cm², then σ = 2000 N ÷ 0.001 m² = 2.0 × 10⁶ Pa = 2.0 MPa.

Electronegativity

The relative tendency of an atom to attract a shared pair of electrons in a bond towards itself.

Lewis structures: show bonding pairs and lone pairs. H follows the duet rule (2 electrons); many main-group atoms follow the octet rule (8).

Lone-pair reminder: PH₃ has one lone pair on central P. HCl’s Cl has three lone pairs; H₂ has none. H₂O’s O has two lone pairs.

Station 05 · Gases

Gas laws & kinetic theory

Gas particles move randomly. Their collisions with the container walls create pressure; their average kinetic energy tracks kelvin temperature.

Station 06 · Reaction patterns

Reaction types & balancing

First recognise the pattern. Then balance with coefficients—never change the subscripts in a formula.

Equation workshop

Reaction pattern cards

CombinationA + B → ABTwo or more reactants form one product.
DecompositionAB → A + BOne compound breaks into simpler products.
Combustion (complete)Hydrocarbon + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂OA fuel reacts with oxygen, releasing energy.
Single displacementA + BC → AC + BA more reactive element replaces a less reactive one.
Double displacementAB + CD → AD + CBIons exchange partners; an insoluble product may precipitate.

Balance in a reliable order

  1. Write correct chemical formulae.
  2. Count each element on both sides.
  3. Adjust coefficients only; balance complex species first.
  4. Leave H and O until later when useful.
  5. Recount every element and simplify coefficients.
Worked example
C₄H₁₀ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O

4HNO₃ → 2H₂O + 4NO₂ + O₂Decomposition · balanced
2Fe + O₂ → 2FeOCombination · balanced in the stated product model

Station 07 · Amount of substance

The mole concept

A mole lets you move between a measurable sample and the number of particles it contains.

Mole converter

One mole, three routes

n = m / M
n = V / Vₘ
n = N / Nₐ

n = amount (mol) · m = mass (g) · M = molar mass (g·mol⁻¹) · V = gas volume · Vₘ = molar volume · N = particle count.

Definition note: The supplied 2021 guide uses the carbon-12 definition. Check your current course memo for the wording expected in an exam. The current SI definition is based on the exact Avogadro constant.

Convert to moles

8 g sulfur ÷ 32 g·mol⁻¹ = 0.25 mol.

Worked example: molar volume at the course STP

Using pV = nRT for 1 mol at 101.3 kPa and 273.15 K:

V = nRT / p
V = (1 × 8.31 × 273.15) / 101300
V ≈ 0.0224 m³ = 22.4 dm³

These STP values follow the convention used in the supplied guide. A different standard-pressure convention gives a different molar volume.

Station 08 · Stoichiometry

Stoichiometry & predicting reactions

Balanced-equation coefficients are mole ratios. The sequence is always: balance → moles → ratio → requested unit.

Ratio calculator

Four-step reaction pathway

  1. Balance the equation.
  2. Convert the known mass or gas volume into moles.
  3. Use the coefficient ratio to find the target moles.
  4. Convert to grams, volume or particles, and include units.
Example: H₂S + Cl₂ → 2HCl + S

8 g S → 8 ÷ 32 = 0.25 mol S. The H₂S:S ratio is 1:1, so 0.25 mol H₂S is needed; at the stated STP this is 0.25 × 22.4 = 5.6 dm³. HCl:S is 2:1, so 0.25 mol S forms 0.50 mol HCl.

Try a mole-ratio conversion

Enter the coefficient of the known substance and the coefficient of the target substance from your balanced equation.

0.25 mol × (2 ÷ 1) = 0.50 mol target.

Reactivity series

K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn
> Fe > Pb > H > Cu > Ag > Au

A more reactive metal can displace a less reactive metal from its compound. Hydrogen is included because past papers compare metals with H₂ too; Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂ is the halogen order for halogen displacement.

Ag + Mg(NO₃)₂: no reaction. Mg + CuSO₄: reaction; Mg displaces Cu.

Conservation of mass

2KNO₃ → 2KNO₂ + O₂
Reactants: 2 × 101 = 202 g
Products: 2 × 85 + 32 = 202 g

Atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed, so total mass is conserved in a closed system.

Station 11 · Past-paper essentials

Hooke’s law & energy diagrams

Both appear in every Paper 2 from 2020 to Nov 2025. Play with them, then explain what you see.

Two mini-labs

Hooke’s law spring

Model: k = 1.5 N·cm⁻¹ with an elastic limit at 10 N. Past the limit the spring stretches permanently and F/Δℓ stops being constant.

Potential-energy diagram

Illustrative values in kJ·mol⁻¹. Exam answer: label axes, reactants, products, Eₐ and ΔH.

Exam-ready points

  • Hooke test: compute F/Δℓ for every row. A constant value means a straight line through the origin, so the spring obeys Hooke’s law (Nov 2025: 3/8 = 6/16 = 0,375 N·cm⁻¹).
  • Graph regions: elastic region → elastic limit → plastic region → breaking point.
  • ΔH = Eₚ(products) − Eₚ(reactants). Exothermic: ΔH < 0. Endothermic: ΔH > 0 (e.g. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂).
  • Activation energy = peak − reactants. A catalyst lowers Eₐ and leaves ΔH unchanged. The activated complex is the unstable top of the curve.
  • Reverse reaction: same peak, but Eₐ(reverse) = peak − products and ΔH changes sign.
Station 12 · Past-paper essentials

Liquids, heat & industrial gases

Section B questions 5, 7 and 9/10 keep returning to these facts.

Exam bank

Liquids and heat

  • Vapour pressure: pressure of a vapour in equilibrium with its liquid. Boiling point: temperature where vapour pressure = atmospheric pressure. Dissolved salt lowers vapour pressure and raises the boiling point.
  • Viscosity falls as temperature rises. Stronger intermolecular forces mean higher viscosity and boiling point.
  • Expansion: ΔV = γV₀ΔT (water γ = 7,5 × 10⁻⁵ °C⁻¹). Example: 100 dm³ heated 22 → 49 °C expands by 0,2025 dm³. Liquids expand more than solids because their particles are held more weakly.
  • Heat: Q = mcΔT; heat lost by the hot object = heat gained by the cold one. Latent heat of fusion: heat to change solid → liquid with no temperature change.
  • Pressure cooker: more pressure → higher boiling point → faster cooking. Absolute pressure = gauge + 101,3 kPa.

  • Acid rain: SO₂ and NO₂ (CH₄ does not cause it). CO binds haemoglobin; H₂S blackens paintings containing lead or silver. Smog: mist of water vapour and pollutants.

Conventions the memos use

  • R = 8,31; STP = 273 K and 101,3 kPa; molar gas volume 22,4 dm³·mol⁻¹. Memos accept 273 or 273,15 for the kelvin conversion.
  • Electronegativity difference below 0,9 → non-polar bond; above → polar.
  • “Show that mass is conserved”: add the molar masses of reactants and of products and show they are equal.
  • Final answers to 2 decimals, with formula, substitution and unit.
